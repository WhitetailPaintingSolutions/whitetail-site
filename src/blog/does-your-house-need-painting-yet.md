People usually call us because the house *looks* tired. That is a fine reason
to paint. But tired and failing are two different things, and only one of them
costs you money if you wait.

Here is what we look at when somebody asks whether it is time.

## Is the old paint letting go?

Walk the outside of the house and look at the places that take the most
weather. South and west walls. Under the eaves. Around window trim.

You are looking for paint that has lifted away from the wood, not paint that
has simply gone dull. Press a thumbnail into a suspicious spot. If a flake
comes away, water is getting behind it, and that is the point where waiting
starts costing you.

Dull paint is a looks problem. Lifting paint is a wood problem.

## Chalk on your hand

Rub your palm firmly across the siding on a sunny wall. If it comes back with
a powdery film on it, the paint has chalked — the surface has broken down under
years of sun.

A little chalk is normal and washes off. Heavy chalk means the coating is near
the end of its working life. It is not an emergency, but it is a real signal,
and it also means whoever paints next has to wash properly or the new coat will
not stick.

## Caulk and end grain

The joints matter more than the flat surfaces. Look where trim meets siding,
where two boards butt together, and at the bottom ends of any vertical trim.

Open gaps there let water into the end grain, which is where wood drinks
fastest. Cracked, shrunken, or missing caulk is worth fixing whether or not you
repaint, and it is cheap compared with replacing a rotten board.

## Inside is a different question

Interior paint almost never fails the way exterior paint does. Inside, you are
painting because you want to, not because you have to.

The exceptions are rooms that take real moisture — bathrooms, laundry rooms,
and the wall behind a stove. If you are seeing flaking above a shower or a
stain that keeps coming back through, that is worth looking at, because
something is usually going on behind it.

## So when is it worth waiting?

If the paint is dull but sound, the caulk is intact, and there is no bare wood
showing, you can usually get another season or two out of it. Wash it and see
how you feel about it clean — a lot of houses that look tired just look dirty.

We would rather tell you that than sell you a job you did not need this year.
The house will still be there next spring, and so will we.

If you are not sure what you are looking at, take a few photos of the worst
spots and send them over. We will tell you honestly what we see.
